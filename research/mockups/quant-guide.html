A guide to studying quantitative finance

Start with probability, statistical inference and programming. These underpin much of quantitative research and interview preparation. Then follow the work that interests you: time series and portfolio construction for systematic research, market microstructure for trading and execution, or stochastic calculus for derivatives pricing. You do not need to finish every subject before tackling useful problems.

This guide recommends a starting resource for each subject, explains what it contributes and identifies when a more specialized text becomes useful. The subject guides assume undergraduate calculus, linear algebra and some programming. If you are preparing for applications, use the separate interview section to choose practice relevant to the role.

Why these subjects belong together

A trading or investment decision begins with an uncertain quantity: a future payoff, a price change, an execution cost or a risk exposure. Probability describes that uncertainty. Statistics addresses what can be learned about it from finite, noisy observations. Optimization converts estimates and constraints into a decision, while programming turns that decision into a procedure that can be tested and repeated. Market knowledge determines whether the assumptions survive contact with actual trading.

The emphasis depends on the work. An empirical researcher needs to distinguish predictive information from selection bias and leakage. A market maker must reason about inventory and the information conveyed by an incoming trade. A derivatives quant needs a coherent pricing model and a reliable numerical implementation. A developer needs to understand the correctness and performance of the systems that support these activities. Use the guide selectively: stochastic calculus is central to some pricing roles, but it is not a prerequisite for every trading interview.

Subject guides

01Probability

Conditional probability is the language of decisions made with incomplete information. A fair value is often a conditional expectation, and a new observation changes the information on which that expectation is based. Independence, correlation and conditional independence are different claims; confusing them can produce a plausible calculation with the wrong model behind it. The foundations should include Bayes' rule, expectation and variance, joint distributions, conditioning and the main limit theorems.

Blitzstein and Hwang's Introduction to Probability is the starting point here. It develops problem-solving techniques alongside the definitions, so you can learn how to set up a problem as well as how to calculate an answer. Its treatment of conditioning, symmetry and indicator random variables helps turn complicated counting or expectation problems into manageable calculations. The official Harvard page links a free online edition and practice material. Work through exercises as you read, and compare alternative derivations rather than checking only the final number; calculus becomes important for continuous distributions.

MIT 18.05 is the next place to look when you need unfamiliar probability and introductory statistics problems. Use its assignments and exams to test whether you can choose a method without the surrounding textbook chapter giving it away. Attempt a problem without the solution, identify whether the difficulty was modeling, algebra or a missing concept, and then return to the relevant explanation. There is no need to follow the entire course to use its problem material.

Think Bayes is an optional computational companion. Turn to its examples and code when you want to calculate and inspect a prior, likelihood and posterior rather than leave the update as a symbolic expression. Use it to build intuition about updating beliefs and predictive uncertainty, while retaining the analytical probability text for derivations. In a finance application, explicitly ask whether the likelihood and the assumed stability of the data-generating process are defensible.

02Statistical inference

A profitable backtest is an observation, not yet evidence of a reliable strategy. How much data supports the result? How dependent are the observations? How many alternatives were tried before this one was selected? Statistical inference gives these questions a mathematical form. Sampling distributions describe how estimates vary; confidence intervals and tests quantify uncertainty under specified assumptions. In financial data, dependence and changing distributions can undermine an analysis that would be reasonable for independent observations.

Wasserman's All of Statistics is the starting reference if you are comfortable with calculus and the probability above. Read it to connect likelihood, estimation, testing, regression and resampling. Its compact presentation requires you to fill in intermediate steps: write those out rather than treating a short derivation as an easy one. A productive use is to derive a simple estimator, simulate its sampling distribution and compare that experiment with the theoretical result.

Casella and Berger's Statistical Inference is the next step when you want deeper derivations of sufficient statistics, estimator properties and tests. You do not need to finish it before fitting a regression; use it when you need to justify the properties of the procedure you are using. MIT 18.650 complements that reading with inference problems and exams. The combination is particularly useful for practising derivations and identifying which assumptions a conclusion depends on.

An Introduction to Statistical Learning provides an applied route into prediction once basic inference is familiar. Work through its labs on regression, regularization and resampling in either R or Python, checking how training performance differs from validation performance. Then use The Elements of Statistical Learning for a more mathematical treatment of the relationships between learning methods. For quant research, the important connection is how model flexibility, regularization and evaluation interact. A more expressive model can fit historical returns better while making the estimate of future performance less reliable.

03Linear algebra and optimization

Linear algebra describes exposures, regressions and covariance; optimization turns objectives and limits into allocations or decisions. In a minimum-variance portfolio, for example, the objective is wᵀΣw, where w contains portfolio weights and Σ is the covariance matrix. Budget, leverage and position constraints determine which solutions are feasible. Understanding positive semidefiniteness and conditioning helps explain both the geometry of the problem and why a numerical solution can be sensitive to estimated inputs.

Boyd and Vandenberghe's Convex Optimization is the main text once projections, eigenvalues and quadratic forms are familiar. Begin with convex sets and functions, then constrained formulations and duality. The aim is to recognize the structure of a problem and interpret its optimality conditions before choosing a numerical method. Read the material on convexity and constrained problems before treating a solver as a black box. The authors provide the book freely, along with additional exercises and links to code examples that connect mathematical formulations to computation.

MIT 18.06 should come first if projections, least squares, eigenvalues or positive-definite matrices are still unfamiliar. Use the notes and problems for the specific gaps you need to close. Those topics recur in regression and risk modeling. After reviewing them, solve the same constrained portfolio problem analytically where possible and numerically with a solver. Inspect active constraints and perturb the covariance estimate; the change in weights often teaches more about the problem than the original optimum.

04Financial time series

Financial observations arrive in time order and can exhibit dependence even when simple return autocorrelations are small. Returns and squared returns can behave very differently: weak predictability in the conditional mean can coexist with strong persistence in volatility. Stationarity assumptions, lag structure and the distinction between conditional mean and conditional variance matter when fitting models and interpreting uncertainty. Randomly shuffling observations can destroy the temporal structure that an evaluation needs to preserve.

Tsay's Analysis of Financial Time Series is the starting text because it develops time-series methods around financial data. Begin after basic regression and probability; follow the progression from return properties to models of the conditional mean and variance. Work from empirical return properties into ARMA and conditional-heteroskedasticity models, checking residual diagnostics and the economic meaning of the specification. Fitting GARCH successfully is only a first step; you should also explain what it predicts and whether its forecasts improve on a simpler baseline.

Hamilton's Time Series Analysis belongs after the applied introduction, when you need a deeper derivation of a dynamic model or its inference procedure. Consult it for the method you are studying rather than making the whole book a prerequisite for your first time-series analysis. Forecasting: Principles and Practice is a useful practical complement for benchmarks, residual checking and forecast evaluation. Its examples use R and its scope is broader than finance; the evaluation habits transfer even when the application and model differ.

The Kenneth French Data Library supplies factor and portfolio-return series with construction notes. It supports exercises in factor regression and time-series behavior without requiring a commercial market-data feed. Verify frequency, units and whether a series represents an excess return or a long–short factor. Fit a model on an earlier interval, evaluate on a later one and preserve that ordering when selecting parameters.

05Derivatives and pricing

How can you price an option without first predicting whether its underlying stock will rise? Begin with replication: construct a portfolio whose terminal payoff matches the option in every state of a simple model. No-arbitrage then ties their prices together. This is the foundation for understanding risk-neutral valuation. Later, Greeks describe how a model price responds to changing inputs; discrete rebalancing, transaction costs and jumps help explain why an actual hedge can leave a residual profit or loss.

Hull's Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives should come first if you need to understand the instruments as well as the mathematics. Start with forwards, futures and option payoffs, then derive put–call parity and a one-period replicating portfolio. Work out the cash flows yourself. These arguments establish what the later pricing models must accomplish and give you simple checks for an implementation.

Shreve's Stochastic Calculus for Finance I is the next step for a mathematical treatment of replication and risk-neutral pricing. Work through the binomial model, conditional expectation and martingale arguments before moving beyond discrete time. Volume II moves to continuous time, including Brownian motion, Itô calculus and continuous-time valuation. Begin it when the pricing problem requires continuous-time machinery. Keep track of what information is available at each time and how that constrains a trading strategy; those details give the stochastic equations their financial meaning.

Glasserman's Monte Carlo Methods in Financial Engineering becomes relevant when you start computing prices by simulation. Use its treatment of estimator efficiency and variance reduction to improve a basic implementation. One estimate landing near the correct price says little about the method’s reliability; examine variation across repeated runs. Compare simulated prices with an analytic benchmark where available, report standard errors and distinguish sampling error from bias introduced by discretizing a process.

06Market microstructure and execution

A quoted price is part of a trading mechanism. A passive order may earn a spread, but it can also be filled precisely when another participant has information that makes the quote unattractive. Inventory creates exposure between trades, and executing a large order changes the balance between market impact and the risk of waiting. These mechanisms explain why a strategy with attractive paper returns can fail once its trades are modeled realistically.

Harris's Trading and Exchanges is the place to begin. Read it to understand who submits orders, why liquidity has a cost and how trading rules shape execution. Before adding a mathematical model, be able to explain why a limit order might remain unfilled and why getting filled can sometimes be bad news. In particular, distinguish spread capture from compensation for inventory exposure and adverse selection.

Cartea, Jaimungal and Penalva's Algorithmic and High-Frequency Trading takes the next step into mathematical execution and market-making models. Read it after Harris and the relevant probability and stochastic-process foundations. The linked publisher preview outlines its coverage. For each model, write down the control problem explicitly: what can the trader choose, how do prices and fills evolve, and what objective is being optimized?

Avellaneda and Stoikov's High-frequency trading in a limit order book is a focused paper on inventory-sensitive quoting under a specified price and order-arrival model. Read it to understand how inventory and risk aversion enter a quoting decision, then reproduce the mechanism in simulation. Compare inventory and profit-and-loss distributions under different assumptions. A simulated improvement demonstrates behavior inside the model; whether the arrival and execution assumptions describe an actual market is a separate question.

07Portfolio construction and systematic research

“Essentially, all models are wrong, but some are useful.”

George Box · Empirical Model-Building and Response Surfaces

Portfolio construction combines forecasts and risk estimates with objectives and constraints. An optimizer can amplify estimation error by assigning extreme weights to small differences in expected returns or covariance. Constraints, regularization and more stable estimates can therefore matter as much as the nominal objective. Evaluating the resulting strategy also requires a record of when inputs became available, how often the portfolio changes and what those changes cost.

Palomar's Portfolio Optimization is the starting text after the optimization foundations above. Use it to turn a portfolio objective into a fully specified problem, including allocation limits and implementation choices. Begin with minimum variance before adding an expected-return forecast whose estimation introduces another source of uncertainty. Reproduce a simple allocation first, then change one element at a time: covariance estimator, weight bounds or the treatment of turnover. Explain how each change affects concentration and stability rather than comparing only the final return.

Coqueret and Guida's Machine Learning for Factor Investing is the next resource when you want to build and evaluate an equity factor strategy. Follow the path from preprocessing through prediction to portfolio construction, using the code and exercises to make each step explicit. Chapters 4, 10 and 12 are particularly relevant to the path from data to an evaluated portfolio. Its focus is factor investing, so it should not be treated as a general guide to high-frequency trading. Use it to examine how a prediction becomes a position and how the evaluation changes after constraints and costs.

Ledoit and Wolf's Honey, I Shrunk the Sample Covariance Matrix addresses the instability of sample covariance estimates through shrinkage toward a structured target. Read it when small changes in your data produce large changes in optimized weights. It connects the covariance estimation problem directly to portfolio construction. Compare sample and shrinkage estimates on the same rolling portfolio experiment and inspect weight instability as well as out-of-sample risk. The French library's portfolio-return datasets provide a possible starting dataset; read how those portfolios were constructed before interpreting the experiment.

Bailey and coauthors' The Probability of Backtest Overfitting examines the selection problem created by trying many strategies and retaining the best-looking one. The related Deflated Sharpe Ratio considers how selection and non-normal returns affect performance assessment. Read these after you can explain an ordinary train/test split. They address a further difficulty: choosing a strategy after seeing the performance of many alternatives changes how the winning result should be interpreted. Keep a record of the hypotheses and parameter choices tried: uncertainty about the search process is itself a limitation when interpreting an impressive backtest.

08Programming and numerical methods

A pricing formula can be correct while its implementation is wrong. A backtest can run successfully while a data join has introduced future information. Learn to check both the algorithm and the data it receives. For research, prioritize reproducible transformations and numerical checks; for development roles, add correctness arguments, complexity and systems behavior. Choose the reading below according to the part of the implementation you need to understand.

Introduction to Algorithms, by Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein, explains data structures and algorithms through correctness arguments and complexity analysis. Use it selectively: start with the data structures, sorting and searching needed for your implementations, then add graph algorithms or dynamic programming as the problems demand. Pair each topic with the CSES Problem Set, whose topic-based collections let you implement related ideas repeatedly. A passing submission is useful feedback, but you should still be able to explain the invariant, worst-case complexity and relevant edge cases.

McKinney's Python for Data Analysis deals with the practical data work surrounding a model: arrays, tabular operations, joins, missing values and time-series handling. Keep it alongside a research implementation and consult the relevant operation as you need it. A join can silently duplicate observations, and a time alignment can leak future information. Reproduce transformations on small examples before relying on a large pipeline; the open third edition makes it easy to consult a specific operation as needed.

Stroustrup's A Tour of C++ is a language overview for readers who already program. Use it to understand C++ abstractions, resource management and the standard library before investigating role-specific performance questions. For a first programming course, his Programming: Principles and Practice Using C++ has a different purpose and a gentler starting point. Designing Data-Intensive Applications complements language study with storage, replication and distributed-system trade-offs. It is relevant to research and trading infrastructure, but does not replace focused preparation for low-latency or operating-systems roles.

For numerical work, return to Glasserman and compare estimators under a fixed computational budget. An antithetic or control-variate estimator may give a more precise result than increasing the number of naive paths. Report the estimator, standard error and runtime together, and use limiting cases or analytical solutions to check the implementation. This connects mathematical understanding with the evidence needed to trust a numerical result.

Interview preparation

“the interview will feel a lot more like a conversation than a quiz”

Jane Street · Trading Interviews

Start with the role description and the firm's official interview material, then choose problems that exercise the relevant skills. For trading, emphasize probability, decisions under uncertainty and clear calculations. For research, add statistical inference, coding and the ability to defend an experiment. For development, concentrate on algorithms, implementation and the systems knowledge the team requires. Pricing roles need their own preparation in valuation and stochastic processes. These areas overlap, but completing every book below would be an inefficient preparation plan.

Question books and mathematical preparation

Xinfeng Zhou's A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews collects problems across probability, calculus, linear algebra, stochastic processes, finance and algorithms. Start here for a broad diagnostic of mathematical preparation. Choose probability and mathematical reasoning problems first, then add the sections relevant to the role. A question you cannot solve should identify something to study; it should not simply become another answer to memorize. Attempt the problem before reading the solution, then identify the reusable method—conditioning, symmetry, a recurrence or a replication argument—and try to reconstruct it later.

Timothy Falcon Crack's Heard on the Street: Quantitative Questions from Wall Street Job Interviews can wait until you need a second source of unfamiliar questions. Work through selected problems in Zhou first; switching books is useful when recognition starts replacing reasoning. Choose questions relevant to the advertised role and construct an argument before checking the solution. Consult the employer’s own material for its interview format.

The university problem collections serve a different purpose. Stat 110 and MIT 18.05 give structured practice in probability and introductory inference; MIT 18.650 pushes further into statistical reasoning. Use them when an interview solution exposes a foundation you cannot derive. For example, if a solution invokes conditional expectation, return to problems that require choosing what to condition on. This is more useful than collecting several explanations of the same remembered puzzle.

Trading: probability, decisions and market reasoning

Jane Street's Probability & Markets is the starting reading for this part of preparation. It connects elementary probability and expectation to making markets, giving a concrete setting for conditional valuation and updating with new information. Work through the examples actively: calculate your estimate, state the uncertainty around it and explain what observation would cause you to update. Then distinguish the estimate of fair value from the price at which you would actually trade, which can depend on inventory, risk and what the other participant might know.

The firm's trading interview page includes an official mock interview showing a problem developing through several parts. Watch how the reasoning changes as the interviewer adds information or changes the question. Pause when a new part is introduced, attempt it yourself and then compare the reasoning. Jane Street explicitly says this process does not require prior finance knowledge or advanced college mathematics; use that information to avoid treating a stochastic-calculus textbook as a universal prerequisite for trading applications.

Zetamac is a configurable arithmetic drill. Use it to reduce errors and hesitation in calculations that would otherwise interrupt a probability or valuation argument. Keep the operation range and time limit consistent when comparing your own sessions, and inspect which calculations cause mistakes. Keep this a short supplement to problem solving. Arithmetic practice is most useful when it removes a specific source of hesitation or error from a longer argument.

Figgie, Jane Street's multiplayer card-market game, can provide practice in interpreting prices and trading with incomplete information. Its value comes from discussing decisions afterward: what did a trade suggest about another player's information, when did your estimate change, and how did your position affect your willingness to trade? Use the probability and microstructure reading to explain those decisions. Without that discussion, another round contributes little to understanding the mechanism.

Research: inference, coding and defending an experiment

Two Sigma's quantitative research and modeling guide identifies data analysis and open-ended problem solving, coding and algorithms, and statistics or a PhD candidate's research domain as areas of discussion. It also links a mock research interview. Use the demo to observe how an initially underspecified question becomes an analysis: what needs to be clarified, which baseline is useful, and what evidence would distinguish competing explanations. Those decisions are worth practising alongside closed-form mathematical questions.

Wasserman is the first revision reference here: return to estimation, tests, regression and resampling wherever a practice question exposes a gap. Practise deriving a simple estimator and explaining its assumptions, then describe what would change under dependence or model misspecification. A regression answer should go beyond naming coefficients: discuss the response, features, residual structure and the meaning of the evaluation. Interview Query provides an additional source of statistics, Python, SQL and data-science practice. Its scope is broader than finance and access varies by question, so select topics deliberately rather than treating the platform as a complete quant-research curriculum.

Prepare a technical explanation of one project you know well. State the hypothesis, the data available at each decision time, the baseline, the train/validation procedure and the choices you made after seeing results. For a trading study, explain universe selection, turnover and cost assumptions; for a machine-learning project, explain target construction and how leakage was prevented. The validation and backtesting material in Coqueret and Guida provides useful questions to ask of your own work. If the idea failed, explain which observation led you to reject it and what you would test next. The project discussion should make your decisions and evidence inspectable.

Development: algorithms, implementation and systems

HRT's engineering interview article explains the importance of programming, data structures, problem solving and systems knowledge, including memory, I/O and process management. It is an account of its 2021–22 campus process, so use it for the skills it describes and confirm the current format with the recruiter. Its discussion of methodical problem solving and communicating changes of approach is particularly useful when practising a coding interview with another person.

CSES groups algorithmic problems by topic, making it useful for building competence in sorting, searching, graphs and dynamic programming. LeetCode is an alternative source when you need more questions; there is no need to maintain parallel progress through both collections. In either case, solve a representative problem, give a correctness argument and analyze time and space complexity before moving on. Test boundary cases such as empty inputs, duplicates and values that stress numeric limits. A fast solution to a familiar question is a weak test of whether you can adapt the technique.

For C++ roles, use A Tour of C++ to review language semantics and resource management alongside actual programming. Discuss object lifetime, ownership, copying and the behavior of the standard containers you use. For infrastructure roles, Designing Data-Intensive Applications helps frame questions about persistence, replication, consistency and failure. The relevant depth depends on the team: a research-data service and a latency-sensitive execution component create different engineering constraints.

Pricing and derivatives roles

For a role that explicitly involves valuation or derivatives modeling, interview preparation should include the ability to derive and interpret basic pricing relationships. Use Hull and Shreve to move from instrument knowledge to a replicating argument. Be able to explain put–call parity, solve a one-period binomial model and distinguish real-world probabilities from risk-neutral probabilities. A formula is much more useful when you can identify which assumption makes it valid and what changes when that assumption is relaxed.

Use the stochastic-process and finance portions of Zhou selectively after those foundations. For continuous-time roles, practise applying Itô's formula and explaining the roles of the drift and diffusion terms. For numerical roles, discuss convergence, bias and variance in a pricing implementation, drawing on Glasserman. Compare results with a known benchmark and explain why a smaller numerical error does not resolve uncertainty about the underlying financial model.

Getting useful feedback from practice

A short example to discuss aloud

You roll a fair six-sided die until the first six. What is the expected number of rolls? Explain your argument without summing an infinite series.

Show a recurrence argument

Let E be the expected remaining number of rolls. You always make one roll. With probability 5/6, you are back in the original situation, so E = 1 + (5/6)E and E = 6. The reset argument relies on independent rolls and an unchanged success probability.

A useful follow-up is to ask for two consecutive sixes. That requires more than one state, because a preceding six changes what counts as success on the next roll.

After reading a worked solution, close the book and reconstruct the argument. Then change an assumption: bias the coin, charge for another roll or introduce dependence between observations. Identify which step changes and why. If you cannot proceed, distinguish between failing to recognize a useful technique and not understanding the underlying concept. The first calls for more unfamiliar problems; the second calls for returning to the theory.

For a mock interview, choose an unfamiliar problem and ask a listener to introduce follow-up questions. State your assumptions, work through a small case and check a first solution before optimizing it. When a hint changes your approach, explain how. Afterward, record the specific obstacle: choosing a model, recalling a result, completing the algebra or communicating the argument. Use that diagnosis to choose the next practice session.

The Jane Street puzzle archive and Project Euler are optional extensions after your preparation covers the role’s main requirements. Choose a problem because you want to investigate its mathematics or implementation, rather than treating completion of either archive as an interview target. Keep the main preparation loop tied to the role: official guidance, relevant problems, a return to the underlying theory, and an attempt to explain the result without relying on a remembered solution.
