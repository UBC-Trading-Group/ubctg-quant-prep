Interview preparation

Start with the role description and the firm’s official interview material. For trading, emphasize probability and decisions under uncertainty; for research, inference, coding and experimental judgement; for development, algorithms, implementation and systems. Pricing roles require valuation and stochastic processes. Use those requirements to choose your reading and practice.

Across roles, prepare to explain your own work. Review the methods and technologies on your résumé: what problem did they solve, why did you choose them, and what were their limitations? Be equally specific about why you want to work in finance and on this team. Connect your interests and experience to the work the role actually involves; conversations with team members can help make that understanding concrete.

Question books and mathematical preparation

Xinfeng Zhou's A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews collects problems across probability, calculus, linear algebra, stochastic processes, finance and algorithms. Use selected questions to diagnose your mathematical preparation, choosing topics relevant to the role. Attempt each problem before reading the solution, then identify the reusable method, such as conditioning, symmetry, a recurrence or a replication argument.

Timothy Falcon Crack's Heard on the Street: Quantitative Questions from Wall Street Job Interviews is an optional second source of unfamiliar questions. Start with Zhou and turn to Crack when you need fresh problems in the areas you are practising.

Trading: probability, decisions and market reasoning

Jane Street's Probability & Markets is the starting reading for this part of preparation. It connects elementary probability and expectation to making markets, giving a concrete setting for conditional valuation and updating with new information. Work through the examples actively: calculate your estimate, state the uncertainty around it and explain what observation would cause you to update. Then distinguish the estimate of fair value from the price at which you would actually trade, which can depend on inventory, risk and what the other participant might know.

The firm's trading interview page includes an official mock interview showing a problem developing through several parts. Watch how the reasoning changes as the interviewer adds information or changes the question. Pause when a new part is introduced, attempt it yourself and then compare the reasoning. Jane Street explicitly says this process does not require prior finance knowledge or advanced college mathematics; use that information to avoid treating a stochastic-calculus textbook as a universal prerequisite for trading applications.

Use Zetamac for short arithmetic drills if calculation speed interrupts your reasoning. Figgie offers practice making decisions with incomplete information; discuss afterward how prices, new information and your position affected your trades. Both are optional supplements to probability and market reasoning.

Research: inference, coding and defending an experiment

Two Sigma's quantitative research and modeling guide identifies data analysis and open-ended problem solving, coding and algorithms, and statistics or a PhD candidate's research domain as areas of discussion. It also links a mock research interview. Use the demo to observe how an initially underspecified question becomes an analysis: what needs to be clarified, which baseline is useful, and what evidence would distinguish competing explanations. Those decisions are worth practising alongside closed-form mathematical questions.

Use the statistical inference section to revisit estimation, tests, regression and resampling wherever a practice question exposes a gap. Practise deriving a simple estimator and explaining its assumptions, then describe what would change under dependence or model misspecification. A regression answer should go beyond naming coefficients: discuss the response, features, residual structure and the meaning of the evaluation. For SQL, PostgreSQL Exercises provides free questions and explanations covering joins, aggregation, subqueries and window functions. Explain how joins affect row counts and how NULL values affect a calculation before applying a query to research data.

Choose one research project you can discuss in depth. Explain the question, your contribution, the baseline and the evidence behind your conclusions. For empirical work, describe what data was available at each decision time and how you separated model selection from evaluation. If a more flexible model performs better across several held-out periods, what evidence would justify choosing it over your simpler model? Also practise proposing a new investigation, including the data you would need, a first test and what would count against the idea. Use the portfolio construction and research guidance to examine the evaluation.

Development: algorithms, implementation and systems

HRT's engineering interview article explains the importance of programming, data structures, problem solving and systems knowledge, including memory, I/O and process management. It is an account of its 2021–22 campus process, so use it for the skills it describes and confirm the current format with the recruiter. Its discussion of methodical problem solving and communicating changes of approach is particularly useful when practising a coding interview with another person.

CSES groups algorithmic problems by topic, making it useful for building competence in sorting, searching, graphs and dynamic programming. LeetCode provides another source of coding problems; use its free questions without needing a premium subscription. There is no need to maintain parallel progress through both collections. In either case, solve a representative problem, give a correctness argument and analyze time and space complexity before moving on. Test boundary cases such as empty inputs, duplicates and values that stress numeric limits. Once you can solve problems correctly, practise unfamiliar variations under a time limit while explaining your reasoning. Aim to implement, test and justify a solution within the interview’s constraints.

Use the programming and systems references above to investigate the languages and tools relevant to the team. For Python, use the official tutorial to review data structures, iterators and generators, and the context-manager reference for resource management. For C++, study object lifetime, ownership and standard containers. For infrastructure work, follow a project’s data through the system: where is it stored, what limits performance, and what happens when a component fails? Be ready to defend an architectural decision from your own work and explain when you would choose differently. A research-data pipeline and a latency-sensitive execution component impose different constraints.

Pricing and derivatives roles

For a role that explicitly involves valuation or derivatives modeling, interview preparation should include the ability to derive and interpret basic pricing relationships. Use Hull and Shreve to move from instrument knowledge to a replicating argument. Be able to explain put–call parity, solve a one-period binomial model and distinguish real-world probabilities from risk-neutral probabilities. A formula is much more useful when you can identify which assumption makes it valid and what changes when that assumption is relaxed.

Use the stochastic-process and finance portions of Zhou selectively after those foundations. For continuous-time roles, practise applying Itô's formula and explaining the roles of the drift and diffusion terms. For numerical roles, discuss convergence, bias and variance in a pricing implementation, drawing on the numerical methods reading. Compare results with a known benchmark and explain why a smaller numerical error does not resolve uncertainty about the underlying financial model.

Getting useful feedback from practice

Worked example · Conditional expectation

You roll a fair six-sided die until the first six. What is the expected number of rolls? Explain without summing an infinite series.

Show the reasoning

Let E be the expected remaining number of rolls. You always make one roll. With probability 5/6, you are back in the original situation.

E = 1 + (5/6)E  →  E = 6

This reset argument relies on independent rolls and an unchanged success probability.

Follow-up

What if you need two consecutive sixes? You now need more than one state, because a preceding six changes what counts as success on the next roll.

After reading a worked solution, close the book and reconstruct the argument. Then change an assumption: bias the coin, charge for another roll or introduce dependence between observations. Identify which step changes and why. If you cannot proceed, distinguish between failing to recognize a useful technique and not understanding the underlying concept. The first calls for more unfamiliar problems; the second calls for returning to the theory.

For a mock interview, ask a partner to vary the wording and introduce follow-ups. Include a project discussion: why did you choose this approach, what alternative did you reject, and what would make you change your decision? Practise giving a concise explanation before going deeper, and explain how new information changes your answer. Afterward, identify the obstacle: understanding the question, choosing an approach, implementing it or communicating the reasoning. Use that diagnosis to choose the next practice session.

The Jane Street puzzle archive and Project Euler are optional extensions once your preparation covers the role’s main requirements. Use them to explore unfamiliar mathematics or implementation problems.
